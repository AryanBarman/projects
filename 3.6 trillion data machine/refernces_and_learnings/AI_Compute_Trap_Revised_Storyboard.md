# The $31.6 Trillion AI Compute Trap
## Revised Visual Storyboard & Motion Direction — Production Edition

**Purpose:** Replace the asset-heavy, instruction-heavy v2 with a clearer, more cinematic and easier-to-produce storyboard. Preserve the existing narration structure and approximate cue timings, but improve visual hierarchy, camera direction, transitions, character performance, environmental motion, asset reuse and factual presentation.

**Delivery:** 1920×1080, 16:9, 2D editorial / restrained 2.5D, mobile-readable.  
**Production principle:** Simple scenes, strong ideas, precise movement. Do not animate everything.

---

# 1. Editorial direction

## The central visual idea

The film is about an enormous AI infrastructure buildout that appears to be about chips, but is actually governed by a chain of recurring costs, locked-up capacity, software dependence, electricity and geopolitics.

Use one recurring visual journey:

**Armored truck → data-center rack → replacement cycle → locked capacity → software tollbooth → power grid → border wall → return to truck.**

The truck is not just an opening prop. It is the visual thread that carries the audience through the story. Its cargo changes meaning as the documentary progresses: first a valuable chip, then a symbol of the capital cycle, and finally a small visible component of a much larger system.

## Tone and visual style

- 2D editorial illustration with restrained 2.5D depth.
- Hand-inked outlines, subtle paper grain and light halftone texture.
- Strong silhouettes and readable shapes; avoid photorealistic 3D objects.
- Characters are simple stickman-like editorial figures with clothing, facial expressions, hands and readable body language.
- Backgrounds should feel like illustrated places: loading docks, restaurant interiors, server halls, construction sites, trading floors, substations and city streets.
- Use perspective and layered scenery to create depth, not excessive geometry.
- Keep the palette controlled: dark teal/ink for infrastructure, warm parchment for historical comparisons, muted steel for inactive systems, and one active accent per shot.
- Do not put a label on every prop. Let the image communicate first; use text only when it adds evidence or clarifies a number.

## Visual hierarchy

Every shot should have:
1. **One hero subject** — the thing the viewer should look at first.
2. **One action** — what changes during the shot.
3. **One supporting detail** — an environmental or secondary motion that makes the world feel alive.
4. **One camera decision** — hold, push, track, pan, orbit or pull back.

A shot may contain many background details, but only one element should dominate. Do not make the viewer read three labels while following a moving character.

## Motion budget

Use three levels of motion:

- **Hero motion:** the important action (door opens, chip travels, rack is replaced, lock closes, cable overloads).
- **Secondary motion:** subtle life (LED pulses, fan rotation, steam, paper movement, blinking monitors, character breathing or shifting weight).
- **Camera motion:** only when it helps reveal, follow or emphasize.

A shot does not need all three. A strong still composition with one carefully timed action is better than constant motion.

## Camera grammar

| Move | Use it for | Direction |
|---|---|---|
| Slow push-in | Suspicion, evidence, a key phrase | 3–8% scale over the shot; ease out gently |
| Fast punch-in | A reveal, contradiction or number | Short 8–15% move, then settle |
| Pull-back reveal | Scale or hidden context | Begin close on the object; reveal the system around it |
| Lateral track | Following a moving subject | Keep subject in a stable screen position |
| Parallax pan | Establishing a location | Foreground moves fastest, background slowest |
| Short orbit | A spatial reveal | Small 10–20° arc; avoid full spins |
| Match move | Connecting ideas | Match shape, direction or motion between scenes |

**Avoid:** arbitrary zooms, repeated camera shakes, constant orbiting, wide shots held too long, and camera movement that competes with text.

## Character performance

Build a small reusable performance library:
- neutral idle with breathing and weight shift
- glance toward a prop or another character
- lean forward / lean back
- open-palm explanation
- pointing / presenting
- hands clasped / pleading
- counting on fingers
- looking down at a document
- surprise recoil
- frustrated shoulder drop
- walking cycle
- seated conversation
- turn-and-look reaction

For each performance, animate **anticipation → action → settle**. Hands should make contact with props. Eyes should lead the head. Do not leave characters in a static pose while the narration describes an action.

## Environmental motion

Keep background movement subtle and selective:
- server fans rotate slowly; LEDs pulse asynchronously
- loading-dock warning light rotates once or twice, not continuously at high speed
- steam drifts upward; flags and cables move slightly
- restaurant lanterns sway very subtly; waiter silhouettes pass in deep background only if needed
- construction crane moves a load slowly; no random machinery motion
- city windows flicker sparingly
- power lines carry a traveling light pulse
- monitors update only when the information matters

## Typography and graphics

- Use large editorial type only for the key proof: `$31.6T`, `4–6 YEARS`, `1% VACANCY`, `5% UTILIZATION`, `$2.3T`, `5M DEVELOPERS`, `POWER`.
- Keep labels short. Prefer one headline and one supporting number.
- Animate charts by drawing the line or growing bars; do not make the whole chart bounce.
- Use number counters only when the number itself is the reveal.
- Give important text a short settle and readable hold. Do not stack multiple moving callouts.
- Keep all essential text within a generous 10% edge margin. Avoid placing captions over faces or the active prop.
- Use consistent units and date labels. Distinguish a forecast from a historical measurement.

## D. Reusable motion clips

Build these as reusable GSAP timelines or equivalent:
- `doorOpenHeavy`: latch rotation, door swing, small bounce, light spill
- `objectReveal`: lid opens, object rises slightly, glow settles
- `chipFlight`: short curved travel with restrained motion trail
- `chipDock`: align, slide into slot, contact flash, LED response
- `characterLean`: torso and head lean, hands follow, settle
- `pleadingHands`: hands clasp, elbows rise, small forward lean
- `clockFastForward`: minute hand sweep with subtle ticking
- `chartDraw`: axis first, then line/bar, then endpoint label
- `lockSnap`: chain/lock closes with small recoil
- `rackSwap`: old rack rolls out, new rack rolls in, cable reconnect
- `paperStamp`: paper lands, stamp hits, slight paper flex
- `meterThrottle`: dial turns down, load bar falls, grid stabilizes
- `powerPulse`: light travels along a cable or transmission line
- `barrierDrop`: barrier descends, stops with weight, dust/impact accent
- `cameraPush / cameraPull / cameraTrack`: standardized camera helpers
- `matchTransition`: outgoing hero shape becomes incoming hero shape

Do not build a bespoke animation for every instance of the same action.

# 3. Revised storyboard — time-locked direction

**Timing note:** The timings below preserve the supplied storyboard's approximate cue structure. The editor must conform the final in/out points to the actual WAV and SRT. If the narration duration differs, retime the shot inside its cue; do not stretch a visual past the next spoken idea. Do not invent extra narration.

## COLD OPEN — 0:00–0:30.5
**Purpose:** Make the audience curious about the cargo, reveal the allocation game, establish the scale, then expose the real bottleneck.

### Shot 1 — The cargo (0:00–0:03.5)
**Narration:** “Look at what is inside this armored truck.”

- **Composition:** Three-quarter rear view of the armored truck dominating the foreground. Loading dock wall, bollards and yellow loading strip establish the place. Keep the truck large enough to read on a phone.
- **Action:** Begin with the truck still. A warning lamp rotates once. Camera tracks a short distance toward the rear doors. Two heavy latches release; the doors swing open with weight.
- **Camera:** Medium-wide start → short push to medium close. No full orbit.
- **Secondary motion:** Small suspension settle; light spills from inside; faint dust in the beam.
- **Sound:** Low engine idle, latch clank, door creak.
- **Assets:** Truck, dock, bollards, warning lamp, rear doors, interior light.

### Shot 2 — Not gold. Not cash. (0:03.5–0:06.5)
**Narration:** “It is not gold bullion and it is not cash.”

- **Composition:** One continuous view into the cargo bay, divided by foreground pallets rather than graphic split-screen.
- **Action:** Camera slides left to gold bars. A clean `GOLD` label appears briefly; a red paper slash crosses it. Slide right to strapped cash; `CASH` appears and is crossed out.
- **Camera:** Short lateral track across the truck bay. Keep perspective consistent.
- **Secondary motion:** Gold glint once; cash strap vibrates as camera passes.
- **Sound:** Two distinct stamp impacts.
- **Text:** Only `GOLD` then `CASH`; no extra HUD.

### Shot 3 — The real cargo (0:06.5–0:12.5)
**Narration:** “According to Cisco's chief information officer, this is how Nvidia's AI chips now travel to AI data centers.”

- **Composition:** Tight insert on a padded shipping case. Foam cutout holds a GPU module.
- **Action:** Case lid opens. Chip lifts just enough to reveal its shape; match cut to the chip moving through the air. The chip crosses a simplified road / landscape and approaches a data-center loading bay. Cut to a close rack slot as it docks.
- **Camera:** Close insert → short tracking follow → punch-in on docking. Avoid a long flying-camera rollercoaster.
- **Secondary motion:** Truck bay light fades; background power lines and campus silhouettes slide in parallax; rack LEDs wake in sequence.
- **Sound:** Foam lid, airy whoosh, precise connector click.
- **Assets:** Case, chip, campus, fence, cooling units, rack module.

### Shot 4 — The allocation dinner (0:12.5–0:18.5)
**Narration:** “In Palo Alto, Oracle's Larry Ellison and Tesla's Elon Musk once spent an hour over sushi at Nobu, literally begging Jensen Huang for a bigger allocation.”

- **Composition:** Warm restaurant booth, medium three-shot. Jensen sits relaxed on one side; two executives sit opposite. Table and sushi are visible but not cluttered.
- **Action:** The two executives lean forward in sequence. One opens his palms; the other clasps his hands. Jensen listens without moving much, then calmly slides one GPU token across the table while keeping the remaining allocation tray near himself.
- **Camera:** Gentle track into the booth; cut to clock insert; return to a tighter table-level two-shot.
- **Time passage:** Clock hand completes one clear sweep; plates accumulate with two quick match cuts, not a stack growing continuously.
- **Character direction:** Eyes move toward the chip; shoulders and hands lead the pleading gesture. Jensen's restrained stillness is the contrast.
- **Sound:** Restaurant room tone, clock tick accelerated, plate slide, chip tap.
- **Assets:** Booth, lantern, clock, sushi plates, cups, chopsticks, three character rigs, allocation tray, chip.

### Shot 5 — The number (0:18.5–0:23.5)
**Narration:** “That scarcity has triggered the largest capital buildout in modern history. $31.6 trillion in global AI data center and infrastructure spending through 2050 according to PwC.”

- **Composition:** Start on the chip on the restaurant table. The table's circular plate becomes a circular construction-site plan via match dissolve. Pull back to reveal a growing data-center campus and cranes.
- **Action:** Campus blocks appear in sequence; one headline number builds after the environment is established: `$31.6T THROUGH 2050`.
- **Camera:** Pull back from macro to wide reveal; settle before the number appears.
- **Secondary motion:** Crane hook moves slightly; one crane load rises; campus lights switch on.
- **Sound:** Music opens up; low construction impacts.
- **Text:** One number and one time horizon. Do not add a second chart here.

### Shot 6 — The trap (0:23.5–0:26.0)
**Narration:** “But unlike railroads or fiber optic cables, this $31.6 trillion buildout is a trap that never stops charging.”

- **Composition:** A completed railway track on the left; a data-center rack on the right. Keep both large and visually simple.
- **Action:** Rail worker drives the final spike; hammer stops. On the right, a rack blade slides out and a replacement blade slides in. An invoice prints from the rack.
- **Camera:** Locked comparison; tiny push toward the active rack on “never stops”.
- **Graphic:** `BUILT` versus `REPLACED`; avoid the vague `ENDS / NEVER STOPS` labels.
- **Sound:** Hammer hit, then repeated printer / mechanical cycle.

### Shot 7 — The real choke point (0:26.0–0:30.5)
**Narration:** “By the end of this video, you will see why 99% of data center space is already spoken for while huge clusters sit nearly empty, and why the real choke point controlling that $31.6 trillion sits outside the server rack altogether.”

- **Composition:** Begin inside a server aisle. A row of racks fills the frame; most bays show reserved tags, while only a few status lights are active. Do not depict every bay with a tiny padlock.
- **Action:** Camera pulls backward through the data-center wall, revealing a substation and transmission lines outside. A single power pulse travels from the grid toward the campus, then stalls at a transformer.
- **Camera:** Pull back through the wall; finish on a strong wide composition with the substation dominant.
- **Text:** `SPACE RESERVED` / `LOW UTILIZATION` should not imply the same metric. End with `POWER IS THE CHOKEPOINT`.
- **Sound:** Rack hum drops away; transformer hum remains. Music transitions to analytical pulse.

# ACT 1 — THE PERPETUAL UPGRADE TRAP
## 0:30–3:00
**Core visual metaphor:** The building shell stays in place while the expensive technology inside is repeatedly replaced. Use the same building cutaway for all three returns.

### Shot 8 — Railways: build once (0:30–0:44)
**Narration:** “Think about how every major infrastructure boom in history has worked. In the 1800s, you laid down steel railroad tracks. And once the line connected New York to Chicago, capital spending collapsed. The asset sat in the ground for decades.”

- **Composition:** Warm parchment landscape, low side view of workers finishing a rail connection. Two station markers establish New York and Chicago without a detailed map.
- **Action:** Worker swings hammer; final rail tie locks in. Camera tracks along the completed track; a simple capex line rises during construction, then flattens as the rail remains.
- **Camera:** Side tracking shot → insert on chart.
- **Secondary motion:** Steam from distant locomotive, small worker movement.
- **Text:** `BUILD → CONNECT → MAINTAIN`.

### Shot 9 — Fiber: bury the network (0:44–0:58)
**Narration:** “In the late 1990s, telecom giants buried millions of miles of fiber optic glass. Once the trench was filled, the building spree was over.”

- **Composition:** Ground-level cutaway of a trench with cable spool and workers.
- **Action:** Fiber line unspools into the trench; shovel pushes soil over it. Match cut from the glowing fiber to a flat line on a chart.
- **Camera:** Short lateral track along the trench; insert on chart.
- **Secondary motion:** Cable spool rotates; soil falls; road surface settles.
- **Text:** `NETWORK COMPLETE`; capex line levels off.

### Shot 10 — AI breaks the old pattern (0:58–1:18)
**Narration:** “But AI infrastructure breaks that law completely. According to PwC's September 2026 global data center outlook, global data center capital expenditure is forecast to climb from roughly $800 billion a year in 2026 to $1.8 trillion a year by 2050. It does not taper off. It accelerates.”

- **Composition:** Historical flatline chart remains on screen. A new line emerges from the right and climbs beyond it.
- **Action:** The flat line cracks / lifts into a rising curve. Camera pushes toward the curve as data-center silhouettes grow behind it.
- **Camera:** Fast punch-in on the break, then steady hold.
- **Graphics:** `$800B / YEAR — 2026` to `$1.8T / YEAR — 2050`. Label as annual capex, not cumulative investment.
- **Secondary motion:** Small campus silhouettes appear along the rising curve; no random cash piles.
- **Sound:** Sharp break, then rising rhythmic pulse.

### Shot 11 — The shell versus the silicon (1:18–1:34)
**Narration:** “Why? Because a data center shell is just concrete, copper, and cooling pipes. The real cost sits inside the racks, and silicon rots on a 2-year clock.”

- **Composition:** Side-on cutaway of the recurring glass-walled data-center shell. Concrete, copper and cooling pipes are static; server racks are bright inside.
- **Action:** Outer shell fades to outline. Camera pushes through to one GPU blade. A calendar flips and the chip's value meter declines.
- **Camera:** Medium cutaway → close insert.
- **Important factual visual correction:** Do not state that silicon literally expires every two years. Show a **technology refresh / competitive obsolescence cycle**, and use PwC's four-to-six-year ICT refresh assumption where the narration permits. If the supplied voiceover explicitly says two years, flag it for script verification rather than visually presenting it as a universal physical lifespan.
- **Text:** `TECHNOLOGY REFRESH` rather than `CHIP EXPIRES`.

### Shot 12 — The replacement cycle (1:34–1:54)
**Narration:** “Boston Consulting Group notes that new GPU generations ship roughly every 2 years, rapidly eroding the residual value of the chips already installed. Therefore, you cannot build an AI data center once and walk away. You have to gut the building and buy the silicon all over again.”

- **Composition:** Same exact shell and camera axis as Shot 11.
- **Action:** Old rack rolls out on rails; cable harness disconnects; new rack rolls in. The building frame does not change. A value tag on the old rack shrinks as the new rack locks in.
- **Camera:** Mostly locked, with a subtle push on “all over again”.
- **Character:** One operator guides the rack, checks a connector, then steps back.
- **Secondary motion:** Cable slack follows the rack; rack LEDs switch from dim to active.
- **Sound:** Wheels, cable disconnect, heavy rack lock.
- **Text:** `SAME BUILDING. NEW EQUIPMENT.`

### Shot 13 — Equipment dominates the spend (1:54–2:04)
**Narration:** “PwC projects that chips and information technology equipment will swell from 70% of data center investment today to 93% by 2050.”

- **Composition:** Reuse the shell cutaway. A clean stacked bar sits beside it.
- **Action:** The equipment portion of the bar expands from 70% to 93%; the building outline remains the same.
- **Camera:** Slow push toward the bar; no camera orbit.
- **Text:** `ICT EQUIPMENT SHARE OF CAPEX: 70% → 93%`.
- **Important:** This is the share of investment, not a claim that 93% of the physical building is filled with chips.

### Shot 14 — Where the capital lands (2:04–2:18)
**Narration:** “Roughly half of that $31.6 trillion—$15.1 trillion—is projected to land in the United States with another $8.2 trillion in Asia Pacific led by China and India.”

- **Composition:** Simplified world map with two regions highlighted. Do not use floating 3D slabs or piles of cash.
- **Action:** Route lines draw from the global total to the US and Asia-Pacific. Region labels appear one at a time.
- **Camera:** Gentle map push; keep geography legible.
- **Graphics:** `US $15.1T` and `ASIA-PACIFIC $8.2T`, clearly marked cumulative through 2050.
- **Secondary motion:** Subtle data pulses along route lines.

### Shot 15 — The utilization question (2:18–3:00)
**Narration:** “But if tech giants are spending $800 billion a year just to keep up with demand, you would assume every single chip inside those buildings is running at 100% capacity 24 hours a day. Right? Look at the utilization logs inside those server rooms because the math breaks down immediately.”

- **Composition:** Server aisle, operator foreground with tablet. Rack rows recede in perspective.
- **Action:** Operator scans racks; camera tracks past active and inactive status lights. Tablet enters foreground and shows a utilization trace that is unexpectedly low.
- **Camera:** Track down aisle → close insert on tablet.
- **Character:** Operator glances from rack to tablet, pauses, then looks back at the rack.
- **Sound:** Fan hum; music thins before the next act.
- **Transition:** Match the tablet grid to the overhead grid of locked capacity in Act 2.

# ACT 2 — 99% FULL, 5% USED
## 3:00–6:15
**Core visual metaphor:** A building can be fully reserved while the machines inside are underused. Keep those two measurements visually separate.

### Shot 16 — Capacity is booked before it exists (3:00–3:18)
**Narration:** “According to Boston Consulting Group's July 2026 market analysis, global data center vacancy has hit a razor thin 1% and roughly 92% of all new capacity is already pre-committed before the concrete is even poured. On paper, it is the tightest real estate market on Earth.”

- **Composition:** Construction site viewed from a slightly elevated angle. Empty concrete pads are visible before racks are installed.
- **Action:** Reserved markers and contract tags attach to the pads before the crane finishes the structure. A small vacancy gauge moves to 1%.
- **Camera:** Slow lateral track over the site; punch-in on gauge.
- **Graphics:** `1% VACANCY` and `92% PRE-COMMITTED` as two separate facts, not a combined graphic.
- **Secondary motion:** Crane hook moves; concrete truck crosses deep background.

### Shot 17 — The utilization contradiction (3:18–3:29)
**Narration:** “But cast.ai utilization logs cited by BCG show some enterprise GPU clusters running at just 5%—99% leased, 5% used.”

- **Composition:** Overhead view of a simple grid of server bays. First show a full row of reserved markers; then reveal that only a small portion of activity indicators are lit.
- **Action:** Music drops to silence briefly. Hold the empty visual for a beat. Then the two metrics appear separately: `99% LEASED` and `AS LOW AS 5% UTILIZATION`.
- **Camera:** Locked overhead; one deliberate punch-in on the active bay.
- **Important:** Make clear this is an example of some clusters, not the utilization of every data center. Vacancy and GPU utilization are different measures with different denominators.
- **Sound:** Silence, then one LED click and groove return.

### Shot 18 — Why buyers hoard compute (3:29–3:40)
**Narration:** “How is that possible? Because AI labs and corporations are terrified of being caught without compute when the next model drops. Therefore, they hoard capacity in long-term opaque bilateral contracts years in advance, paying for massive clusters that sit largely idle just to insure against missing out.”

- **Composition:** Two buyers stand beside a row of dark racks. Contract sheets stretch across the floor like reserved parking tickets.
- **Action:** Buyer one locks a rack bay; buyer two adds another contract. The rack remains mostly dark. A small “next model” notification lights up in the distance and both buyers look toward it.
- **Camera:** Medium two-shot → track along the locked bays.
- **Character:** Nervous glance, quick signature, lock action, then a small relieved exhale.
- **Sound:** Pen scratch, lock snap, distant alert.
- **Text:** `BUYING INSURANCE AGAINST SHORTAGE`.

### Shot 19 — Flat-rate protection disappears (3:40–3:52)
**Narration:** “Meanwhile, the bill is quietly shifting onto everyone else. For years, flat rate subscriptions insulated enterprise buyers from what AI compute actually cost. That era ended in the spring of 2026.”

- **Composition:** Developer at desk; a simple flat monthly subscription card acts like an umbrella shielding the user from a rising compute-cost meter.
- **Action:** Cost meter climbs behind the umbrella. Umbrella begins to bend, then folds. The developer notices the bill.
- **Camera:** Medium close, slow push as the shield fails.
- **Secondary motion:** Desk monitor glow, hand pauses over keyboard.
- **Text:** `FLAT FEE → METERED USAGE`.

### Shot 20 — The meter reaches the user (3:52–4:12)
**Narration:** “Between March and April 2026, Windsurf imposed hard weekly limits. OpenAI moved Codex to token metering. GitHub tightened Copilot caps. And Anthropic shifted enterprise customers to a $20 per seat base fee plus pay-per-token rates, explicitly blaming the deepening crunch for AI compute.”

- **Composition:** Four clean editorial cards arranged in a 2×2 grid. Each card has one recognizable product wordmark treatment or neutral product label, a simple meter icon and one short change.
- **Action:** Cards enter one by one; meter fills or cap closes. Keep each card still once its information is readable.
- **Camera:** Locked graphic composition; no zoom on every tile.
- **Graphics:** Use only the exact changes supported by the source and narration. Avoid implying all plans or all users were affected identically.
- **Sound:** Four distinct UI ticks, not four loud impacts.
- **Production note:** Verify the exact date, plan and wording for each provider before final export.

### Shot 21 — Compute becomes a market (4:12–4:32)
**Narration:** “BCG projects the total market for AI computing power will surge from $360 billion in 2025 to roughly $2.3 trillion by 2030. And when a market hits $2.3 trillion with wild price swings—H100 rental rates crashed from roughly $8 an hour in early 2024 to $1.96 in late 2025 before snapping back to $2.64 in April 2026—Wall Street shows up.”

- **Composition:** One editorial market chart fills the frame. A small rack silhouette sits behind the chart as a visual anchor.
- **Action:** Market-size bars grow from $360B to the projected $2.3T. A separate price line drops and rebounds. Trader silhouettes enter only after the chart establishes the opportunity.
- **Camera:** Slow push to chart; lateral reveal to traders.
- **Graphics:** Clearly distinguish **market size projection** from **GPU rental price history**. Label the price series with its index/provider and contract type if known.
- **Fact check:** The exact H100 price sequence is methodology-sensitive. Do not present it as a universal spot price without naming the index, GPU variant, rental term and date.
- **Sound:** Chart draw, ticker begins, footsteps.

### Shot 22 — The market lacks a common ruler (4:32–4:48)
**Narration:** “Independent benchmarks, including the Ornn H100 index, the Silicon Data H100 rental index, and the Compute Desk Hopper US index, have already begun tracking GPU hours on Bloomberg terminals, though their methodologies still diverged by up to 41% over 19 months.”

- **Composition:** Three vertical index panels, each with a clearly different line style. A single bracket indicates the divergence.
- **Action:** Lines draw at slightly different rates; bracket appears last.
- **Camera:** Locked frame, tiny push toward the divergence bracket.
- **Text:** `THREE INDEXES. THREE METHODOLOGIES.` then `UP TO 41% DIVERGENCE` only if the cited study and period substantiate it.
- **Sound:** Three light data ticks; one low accent when the bracket appears.

### Shot 23 — The value of a liquid market (4:48–5:08)
**Narration:** “BCG estimates that turning opaque GPU rentals into a liquid, tradable market, complete with standardized benchmarks, secondary resale, and futures contracts, could unlock up to $140 billion a year in dark value. That combines up to $97 billion in annual trading arbitrage with roughly $26 billion a year in interest savings simply by letting lenders hedge chip depreciation and cut borrowing rates by nearly two full percentage points.”

- **Composition:** A locked crate labeled `GPU CAPACITY` sits between a lender and a compute operator. Behind them, two transparent value streams flow into a central market.
- **Action:** Standardized benchmark tag attaches to crate; secondary-market arrows appear; futures contract slips onto the table. Two value columns rise and combine into the headline.
- **Camera:** Medium composition → close on the two value streams.
- **Graphics:** Show the cited components exactly as reported. Do not imply `$97B + $26B = $140B`; the arithmetic does not equal the headline. Label the headline as “up to” and visually separate the cited components from the total estimate.
- **Sound:** Lock opens, paper slide, soft financial pulse.

### Shot 24 — Compute cannot travel like oil (5:08–5:20)
**Narration:** “But here is the catch that breaks the 'AI is the new oil' comparison. Oil moves on tankers and pipelines. So a barrel in Houston trades within a few dollars of a barrel in Rotterdam. AI compute cannot move like oil. Workloads must sit near their data and run at the speed of light. A GPU in Virginia cannot easily replace a GPU in Frankfurt. Like wholesale electricity, AI compute is trapped by geography.”

- **Composition:** A single frame with two visual lanes. Left: tanker moves freely across an open sea route. Right: a GPU rack sits inside a regional data center connected to local data lines.
- **Action:** Oil route line travels smoothly between ports. Compute route tries to cross the ocean but is interrupted by a latency / data-location boundary. Show the workload staying local.
- **Camera:** Track with tanker, then match movement to the compute route stopping.
- **Graphics:** `OIL: SHIPPED` / `COMPUTE: LOCATION-BOUND`.

Use transitions that carry meaning rather than applying the same wipe everywhere.

# 5. Editing and pacing rules

1. **Do not split every sentence mechanically.** Split when the idea changes, the visual action changes or a reveal needs a separate beat.
2. **Keep a visual anchor.** Every scene should have one object or character that holds the composition together.
3. **Vary shot length.** Use short inserts for evidence, medium shots for character action and longer holds for major metaphors.
4. **Do not overuse close-ups.** A close-up is useful only if the detail matters. Follow it with a wider shot when spatial understanding is needed.
5. **Do not animate every number.** One animated number per proof moment is enough.
6. **Use silence selectively.** Reserve it for the 99%-leased / 5%-used contradiction and the electricity reveal.
7. **Let actions finish.** A lock should close, a rack should dock, a character should settle. Avoid cutting before the viewer understands the action.
8. **Avoid generic stock motion.** No random floating particles, constant pulsing outlines, repeated camera shake or endless moving HUD.
9. **Use sound to support contact.** Every important object interaction should have a matching sound: latch, paper, cable, wheel, lock, connector, transformer.
10. **Keep end-screen space clean.** The final 13 seconds should not contain important visual information behind end-screen cards.

# 6. Production implementation notes

## Layer structure

For each scene, use a consistent layer order:

1. Background sky / wall / distant skyline
2. Far environment (buildings, towers, distant racks)
3. Mid environment (main room, floor, road, machinery)
4. Hero props
5. Characters
6. Foreground framing objects
7. Lighting / shadows / texture overlays
8. Typography and data graphics

Use parallax only when the camera moves. A static shot does not need every layer drifting.

## Camera and animation architecture

- Keep camera movement in one parent transform or camera controller; do not independently animate every layer to fake a camera move.
- Use world-space positions for characters and props, with a consistent ground plane.
- Use reusable GSAP timelines for repeated actions.
- Separate character pose animation from scene choreography.
- Use one timeline per shot, with named labels for narration beats and sound cues.
- Keep asset names stable and descriptive: `truck_rear_open`, `gpu_module_active`, `rack_row_01`, `substation_main`, `cuda_gate`, `border_barrier`.
- Preload assets and verify they are actually rendered in the relevant shot. A loaded asset that is never placed in the scene is not a completed asset integration.
- Avoid oversized transparent PNGs that cover the entire stage; crop assets to their visible bounds.
- For SVG assets, keep important elements separated when they need independent motion.

## Minimum animation acceptance test

Before approving a shot, verify:

- Hero subject is readable on a phone-sized screen.
- The main action is obvious without reading the caption.
- Character hands visibly contact props when required.
- Camera motion has a clear start, destination and settle.
- Background parallax does not distract.
- Text is not covered by characters or props.
- Every required asset is visibly integrated in the shot.
- No important object pops in or disappears without an intentional transition.
- The shot's final composition leads naturally into the next shot.
- Audio cue and visual action land together.

# 7. Research and factual corrections to resolve before final animation

These are important because a visually polished documentary can still lose credibility if the graphics turn qualified claims into absolute facts.

| Storyboard claim | Research / production note | Visual treatment |
|---|---|---|
| $31.6T through 2050 | PwC's central scenario is cumulative data-center capex through 2050, with a range of roughly $22T–$50T. | Label as a projection / central scenario, not guaranteed spending. |
| Annual capex $800B to $1.8T | PwC gives annual data-center capex estimates for 2026 and 2050. | Show annual units clearly. |
| Chip refresh every 2 years | PwC's outlook describes ICT equipment refreshes on a four-to-six-year assumption; the supplied narration cites faster GPU-generation cadence. | Separate product-generation cadence from useful-life / replacement assumption. Do not depict literal expiry. |
| ICT share 70% to 93% | PwC describes the share of total capex represented by ICT equipment. | Show a share-of-investment bar, not a building fill meter. |
| 1% vacancy / 92% pre-committed / 5% utilization | BCG reports these as separate measures; the 5% figure refers to some enterprise GPU clusters and is attributed to Cast.ai. | Keep separate labels and denominators; do not imply all facilities are 5% utilized. |
| $2.3T compute market | BCG's recent research describes a 2030 revenue requirement / payer-side market model around this level; exact framing matters. | Label using the precise BCG definition; do not casually call it a simple market forecast. |
| H100 $8 → $1.96 → $2.64 | GPU rental prices differ by index, GPU configuration, region, rental term and provider. | Name the index and methodology on screen or in a source note. Do not present as universal price. |
| $140B dark value | BCG frames this as a potential value opportunity. The cited $97B and $26B components do not arithmetically sum to $140B. | Present components separately; do not use an additive animation that implies they equal the headline. |
| CUDA developer / market / TOP500 figures | Keep each figure tied to its source and date. | Show as dated claims, not timeless facts. |
| $5T valuation and FY26 revenue | Market capitalization and annual revenue are different measures. | Put them in separate callouts and verify the exact date and reporting basis. |
| China procurement restriction | The September 2025 report describes a reported directive affecting specified Nvidia China-specific chips and named firms. | Use narrow wording such as “reported procurement restrictions”; do not imply a blanket ban on all Nvidia chips. |
| Export-control scenario | PwC distinguishes the annual 2030 impact from the cumulative 2026–2050 shortfall. | Use separate visuals and units. |
| Aurora / grid software | Confirm the project name, partners, date and exact technical role before describing it as a deployed facility or guaranteed grid safeguard. | Show flexible load management only to the extent verified. |

# 8. Final creative rule

**Keep the scene simple. Make the action meaningful. Move the camera only when the audience needs to discover something.**
